Investment banking and stock trading connect businesses that need capital with investors who supply it. A stock represents partial ownership in a company, with potential returns from dividends and rising share prices, alongside the possibility of losses. The SEC’s introduction to stocks establishes this foundation, while Société Générale’s overview explains how investment bankers arrange financing and advise on mergers and acquisitions. An initial public offering, or IPO, connects these activities: underwriters help manage a company’s public offering, after which investors can trade its shares. The SEC’s IPO bulletin emphasizes studying the prospectus and distinguishing the offering price from subsequent trading prices. Behind these transactions lies a productive business. Warren Buffett captures this perspective: “Focus on the future productivity of the asset you are considering.” His approach begins with understanding what an asset can earn. (Berkshire Hathaway, 2013 shareholder letter)

Understanding a business requires learning its financial language. The SEC’s financial statements guide explains how the balance sheet records assets and obligations, the income statement measures profitability, and the cash flow statement tracks cash movements. Together, these statements reveal why reported profit and available cash can differ. Analysts then use this information to estimate value. In Aswath Damodaran’s NYU valuation materials, discounted cash flow analysis translates expected future cash flows into present value using a discount rate appropriate to their risk. More advanced analysis examines reinvestment, terminal value beyond the explicit forecast period, and the weighted average cost of debt and equity financing. Relative valuation compares companies through multiples, while acquisition analysis evaluates whether anticipated benefits justify the purchase price. These methods develop Benjamin Graham’s distinction, repeated by Buffett: “Price is what you pay, value is what you get.” The market supplies a price; analysis supplies an estimate of value. (Berkshire Hathaway, 2013 shareholder letter)

Trading introduces another set of decisions: how, when, and at what cost to act. The SEC’s guide to order types explains that market orders seek immediate execution without fixing the execution price, while limit orders specify an acceptable price but may remain unfilled. A stop order becomes a market order when triggered, so its eventual execution price can differ from its stop price. At the institutional level, CFA Institute’s public summary on trade execution examines liquidity, bid–ask spreads, market impact, algorithms, and implementation shortfall—the difference between actual results and a theoretical portfolio traded at decision prices. Financing also matters: the SEC’s margin account bulletin shows how borrowing magnifies outcomes and can produce forced sales or losses exceeding the original investment. Uncertainty therefore belongs within the analysis. As Howard Marks writes, “The important thing is to recognize that cycles reverse, and to allow for it.” (Oaktree, 2001 memo)

Sound financial judgment also requires understanding portfolios and investor behavior. In his Yale lecture, Robert Shiller explains how diversification depends on the relationships among investments: assets that move differently can reduce portfolio risk. The capital asset pricing model connects expected returns with exposure to market risk, while the Sharpe ratio evaluates returns relative to volatility. These concepts help distinguish investment skill from returns achieved by assuming greater risk. Behavioral evidence adds another dimension. Barber and Odean’s study of 66,465 households found that frequent trading was associated with weaker returns after transaction costs; the authors identified overconfidence as a plausible explanation. Their evidence comes from a historical sample spanning 1991–1996, which matters when interpreting its results. The paper opens with Graham’s observation: “The investor’s chief problem—and even his worst enemy—is likely to be himself.” Throughout this course, we will connect financial analysis, valuation, execution, and risk management with the discipline needed to make informed decisions.

Part 1: The Stock Market and Investment Banking

Two-page summary. The ten numbered concepts below are the learning targets for your eventual quiz. After reading, we’ll stay in the question-and-answer stage until you say “Done.”

Imagine a fictional business called Campus Coffee. Its founders want to open additional locations, but expansion requires money for equipment, buildings, inventory, and employees. Investors may be willing to provide that money in exchange for ownership or a financial claim against the business. Investment banking helps arrange financing and corporate transactions; stock trading allows investors to buy and sell ownership interests. Following the money will help you understand how these activities connect.

1. A stock represents ownership in a company.

Stocks are also called equities. Purchasing shares makes you a shareholder: you own a fraction of the company. If Campus Coffee has one million shares outstanding and you own 1,000, your ownership stake is 0.1%. Common shares generally provide voting rights, although the rights attached to particular share classes can differ. Ownership does not guarantee a positive return. (SEC: Stocks)

2. Stock returns can come from dividends and price appreciation.

A dividend distributes money to shareholders. Capital appreciation occurs when a share’s price increases. A company may retain earnings to fund growth instead of distributing dividends. Prices can also fall, producing losses. When evaluating a stock, consider both distributions and changes in its price, along with the costs of investing. (SEC: Stocks)

3. Common and preferred shareholders have different rights.

Common shareholders generally vote on corporate matters. Preferred shareholders usually have priority over common shareholders for dividends and distributions if the company is liquidated. Bondholders are paid before shareholders. Common shareholders receive what remains after higher-priority claims are satisfied, which may be nothing. These differences explain why the type of security matters. (SEC: Stocks)

4. The primary market raises money through newly issued securities.

The primary market is where an issuer sells new securities to investors and receives the proceeds. The issuer is the organization creating the security.

Suppose Campus Coffee issues 100,000 new shares for $10 each. Investors pay $1 million, and the company receives that money before offering expenses. Campus Coffee can then use the proceeds for its stated purposes.

Primary-market financing includes newly issued stock and newly issued bonds. A company does not have to be conducting its first public offering for a transaction to involve the primary market. The defining features are the new securities and the proceeds flowing to the issuer. (SEC: Primary Market)

5. The secondary market transfers existing securities between investors.

The secondary market is where investors buy and sell securities that already exist.

Suppose you bought 100 Campus Coffee shares and later sold them to another investor for $12 each. The buyer pays you $1,200 before transaction costs. Campus Coffee receives no new financing from that particular resale.

This distinction explains why buying a company’s publicly traded stock usually does not mean sending money directly to that company. You are commonly purchasing an existing owner’s shares. The business remains the issuer, but ownership changes hands between investors. To classify a transaction, identify whether the securities are newly issued or already outstanding, then trace who receives the proceeds. (SEC: Secondary Market)

6. Investment banks help organizations raise capital.

Capital is money committed to financing a business or other activity. Investment bankers help clients arrange equity and debt financing.

Equity financing raises money through ownership interests. Debt financing raises borrowed money through instruments such as bonds. Investment banking teams specializing in these activities are often called equity capital markets, or ECM, and debt capital markets, or DCM.

Campus Coffee might consider issuing shares or arranging debt financing for its expansion. The investment bank helps structure and execute the financing transaction. Its corporate client’s financing needs guide the assignment. (Société Générale: Investment Banking)

7. Investment bankers also advise on mergers and acquisitions.

A merger combines businesses; an acquisition involves purchasing a business or ownership interest. These activities are commonly grouped as M&A.

If Campus Coffee wants to acquire a smaller coffee chain, investment bankers may advise on the transaction. Other specialized teams can help arrange acquisition financing, including combinations of debt and equity.

Investment banking therefore includes both raising capital and advising on major corporate decisions. A banker advising Campus Coffee’s acquisition and an investor trading its shares are performing different activities, even though both concern the same company. (Société Générale: Investment Banking)

8. An IPO introduces a company’s shares to public investors.

An initial public offering, or IPO, generally marks a company’s first public share offering. Investment banks serving as underwriters manage and sell the offering. Some IPOs also include shares sold by existing owners. Proceeds from those owners’ sales go to them; proceeds from newly issued company shares go to the company. (SEC: Investing in an IPO)

9. An IPO’s offering price can differ from its trading price.

The company and underwriters determine an offering price through analysis, negotiation, and indications of investor demand. Once public trading begins, shares may trade above or below that price.

If Campus Coffee’s offering price is $10 but you purchase shares in public trading at $14, your purchase price is $14. Your investment’s subsequent return depends on what you actually paid. (SEC: Investing in an IPO)

10. Disclosure supports analysis; SEC review does not guarantee investment quality.

The prospectus describes the company, offering terms, risks, finances, and planned use of proceeds. Investors should examine it before evaluating an IPO.

SEC review focuses on disclosure requirements. A registration statement becoming effective does not mean the SEC recommends the investment, guarantees its accuracy, or promises that investors will earn a profit. (SEC: Investing in an IPO)
