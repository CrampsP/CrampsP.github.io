3. Deduplicating Training Data — Lee et al. (2022). Their experiments establish benefits from deduplication under the conditions they tested. Full paper

Additional experiments favored response quality and prompt diversity over simply increasing example counts. Nevertheless, the authors reported weaknesses under adversarial prompts and acknowledged that constructing excellent examples requires considerable effort. Full paper
5. Documenting C4 — Dodge et al. (2021). Auditing C4 revealed unexpected sources, machine-generated material, benchmark contamination, and unequal effects from filtering. A word blocklist disproportionately excluded documents associated with minority dialects and identities. The paper shows why evaluating data quality requires examining both retained and discarded material. Full paper
Where the papers agree
- Selection matters alongside quantity. DataComp-LM, FineWeb, and LIMA experimentally demonstrate benefits from choosing useful examples. Large language models (LLMs) can produce fluent responses, summarize complex information, and assist with tasks ranging from writing to programming. Yet fluency alone does not establish that a model is accurate, useful, or trustworthy. These qualities depend in substantial part on the data from which the model learns. Large training datasets may contain repetitive passages, unreliable information, and uneven representation of different communities and perspectives. Increasing the amount of text therefore does not automatically address weaknesses in its quality. Understanding LLM performance requires examining how researchers select training material, remove unnecessary repetition, and preserve valuable information during filtering. It also requires evaluating whether improvements on academic benchmarks translate into useful and responsible behavior. Good LLMs mean good data: effective and trustworthy language models depend on carefully selected, cleaned, and representative training data.
